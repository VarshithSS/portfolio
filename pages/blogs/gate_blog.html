My GATE Journey:
What Worked, What Didn't

AIR 403. A good rank on paper, a hard shift by luck, and a result that left me both satisfied and restless. Here's everything I'd tell myself if I could start over.

the beginning

I started preparing for GATE in August 2024. The generally accepted wisdom is to start by June and give yourself a full seven months. I gave myself five. At the time I didn't think two months would matter that much. Looking back, those two months showed up everywhere — in the rushed syllabus completion, in the compressed revision window, and ultimately, on exam day.

That said, I don't regret how I structured the preparation once I actually started. The strategy was sound. The execution had cracks.

the preparation plan

I broke the entire preparation into three distinct phases, and sticking to this structure was probably the best decision I made.

Aug – Dec
2024
Phase 1 — Syllabus Completion
Topic by topic: watch lectures, make handwritten notes, then immediately solve PYQs for that topic before moving on. I bought a dedicated PYQ book, though online resources work just as well. The key was never moving to the next topic without solving questions from the current one — that's what converts passive understanding into actual recall.
Jan 2025
~30 days
Phase 2 — Subject-wise Revision
3 days per subject, 10 subjects, 30 days. In those three days: revisit the notes I made in Phase 1, condense them into short revision notes, then solve PYQs a second time. After that, take topic-wise and full subject tests from a test series. The short notes you build here become your best friend in the last week.
Feb 2025
~30 days
Phase 3 — Mock Test Phase
Mixed-subject tests and full-length mock tests, one of each per day. Target: at least 10 full tests before exam day. After every test, analyse every wrong answer. If a question had a clever approach you hadn't seen, note it down. These collected approaches are worth more than any single revision session.
Last week
Phase 4 — Final Revisit
No new material. Go through your short notes, revisit the approaches you collected, and let things settle. Rest matters here more than you think.
mistakes I made

I'll be direct about this — knowing these going in would have changed my outcome.

  • ⚠ Late start. Beginning in August instead of June meant my Phase 1 ran well into December. Ideally, syllabus completion should finish by November so you have a full two months for revision and mocks. Mine finished towards the end of December, leaving everything compressed after that.
  • ⚠ Three full mock tests per day. This is the one I regret the most. During the mock test phase, I was giving three full-length tests a day thinking more exposure meant better preparation. It doesn't. You can't properly analyse three full tests in a day — you end up skimming the review or skipping it entirely. And after a few days of this, the burnout compounds. By the time the exam arrived, I was running on empty.
the real cost

Three mocks a day meant no time to analyse mistakes and a burned-out mind on exam day. One well-analysed mock beats three skimmed ones every single time.

exam day

The paper was lengthy. Not hard — lengthy. The questions themselves were straightforward, but there were enough of them that time pressure became real very quickly. I panicked. And when you panic in GATE, you don't just slow down — you start second-guessing questions you actually know. I made mistakes on problems I could have solved cold any other day.

I was expecting 75+ marks. I scored 72. On a different day, with a calmer head, those 3 marks were absolutely there for the taking.

shift luck

My shift turned out to be significantly harder than others. Normalized scoring accounted for this — and honestly, a hard paper in a well-normalised exam can work in your favour if you hold it together. The candidates who did well in hard shifts were the ones who stayed composed and played the long game, not the ones who tried to sprint through every question.

AIR 403 is a good rank. I know that. And part of me is genuinely satisfied — especially given how hard my shift was. But another part of me keeps going back to 2–3 specific questions where I panicked and picked the wrong option. Questions I knew. That's the part that stays with you. Not the rank — the feeling that you left something on the table.
what I'd do differently
  • → Start in June. Seven months is the right duration. Five months is survivable but you'll feel it.
  • → Finish syllabus by November. This gives you a real two-month buffer for revision and mocks — not a compressed one.
  • → One full mock per day, not three. Spend the same total time but divide it: one test, then thorough analysis. That's the loop that actually improves your score.
  • → Collect good approaches. When you see a clever solution during analysis, write it down. Those collected insights compound over weeks.
  • → Be calm in the exam. This sounds obvious until you're sitting there with a lengthy paper and a ticking clock. The students who score well aren't always the ones who know the most — they're the ones who execute calmly under pressure. Breathe. You've put in the work. Trust it.
the one thing

If I had to pick a single lesson: calm and composed beats cramming and panic every time. GATE rewards consistency and clear thinking — not last-minute heroics. The preparation is the easy part. Learning to stay in control when it counts is the real challenge.
